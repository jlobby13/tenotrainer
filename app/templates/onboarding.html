When should you reassess? — What the evidence says

6–8 weeks

The standard interval recommended in tendinopathy rehabilitation literature. Sufficient time for measurable neuromuscular adaptation and tendon structural remodelling to occur in response to progressive loading. Reassessing earlier will typically show minimal change and may lead to premature stage adjustments.

Sooner if…

A significant change in symptoms has occurred — marked worsening, a new acute episode, suspected structural injury, or a clinician has asked you to repeat the assessment. Pain profile changes or inability to tolerate previously manageable loads are valid triggers for early reassessment.

What changes

A new assessment will update your irritability classification, rehab stage, exercise dosage, session plan, and VISA-A score. If you have progressed, this will unlock higher-load exercises and revised targets. If symptoms have worsened, the plan will adjust accordingly. VISA-A and functional data are tracked together on the dashboard.

{% endif %} {# Clinical indicators and rising likelihood #}

Why these symptoms matter — rising likelihood of rupture:

Audible pop / snap

A sudden sharp sound at the back of the ankle during activity is one of the most reliable signs of complete Achilles rupture. Patients often describe it as feeling like they were struck or kicked. High specificity for full-thickness tear.

Cannot plantarflex

Inability to point the foot downward against gravity or resistance suggests the Achilles is no longer mechanically continuous. Partial tears may retain some plantarflexion strength; complete rupture typically eliminates it. Strongly raises suspicion of complete rupture.

Cannot bear weight

Inability to load the affected leg indicates significant structural compromise. While isolated tendinopathy rarely prevents weight-bearing entirely, rupture — complete or high-grade partial — frequently does. Significantly elevates clinical suspicion.

Palpable gap

A visible or palpable depression in the tendon 2–6 cm above the heel indicates a physical discontinuity in the tendon tissue. Early haematoma and swelling may obscure this finding within hours of injury — its presence when detected is highly diagnostic of rupture.

Bruising / swelling

Rapid, diffuse bruising and swelling around the heel and lower leg following acute onset pain suggests significant tissue trauma and haemorrhage. This pattern is more consistent with structural injury than tendinopathy, which typically presents with localised, gradual-onset tenderness.

Thompson test

The Thompson (Simmonds) test — lying prone with the foot free, squeezing the calf — is a validated clinical screen. A positive result (foot does not plantarflex) has sensitivity ~96% and specificity ~93% for complete Achilles rupture and is the standard bedside test used in emergency and orthopaedic settings. Multiple co-occurring indicators above substantially increase the probability of a clinically significant tear.

{# Action directive #}
Immediate action required: Present to an emergency department or urgent orthopaedic clinic. Do not attempt to walk, run, or load the affected leg until assessed. Early diagnosis determines whether conservative management or surgical repair is appropriate — delay worsens outcomes for complete ruptures.
